Why We Celebrate

Ancient roots

Deepavali, from the Sanskrit for “row of lamps”, is one of India’s oldest festivals. It began as a harvest celebration at the end of the monsoon, when homes were cleaned and lit to welcome prosperity.

Rama returns to Ayodhya

According to the Ramayana, Lord Rama came home after fourteen years of exile and his victory over Ravana. The people of Ayodhya lit rows of earthen lamps to light his way.

Krishna and Narakasura

In South India the festival remembers Krishna defeating the demon Narakasura, freeing thousands of captives. The victory of light over darkness is the same.

Goddess Lakshmi

Many families, especially in Maharashtra and Gujarat, honour Lakshmi, goddess of wealth, on the main night. Doors stay open and lamps burn so she may enter.

Beyond Hindu tradition

Jains mark Mahavira’s nirvana, Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas for Guru Hargobind’s release, and some Buddhist communities also take part. Today it is celebrated around the world.

The Five Days

Vasu Baras

In Maharashtra, cows and calves are worshipped to begin the festival.

Dhanteras

People honour Dhanvantari and Lakshmi, and buy something new, often gold or utensils.

Naraka Chaturdashi

Early oil baths, fresh sweets and the first lamps. Many call this Choti Diwali.

Lakshmi Puja

The main night: rangoli at the door, diyas in every window, prayers, fireworks and family.

Bhai Dooj

Sisters apply tilak to their brothers and pray for their long lives. A day for siblings.
